# Atlas of Influence — Framework Spec

This file is the source of truth for the model (decided 13 Nov 2026; it began as a copy of an earlier working document). `../data/framework.json` mirrors its structure for the framework map. Evidence lives in `../data/evidence.json`.

## Purpose and scope

The Atlas of Influence traces how actors, institutions and machines shape what content reaches a phone screen, and what it does to the person reading it. It is a learning tool: every claim is a sourced path through a shared model, not an accusation.

**Scope** (decided 13 Nov 2026). The Atlas lets a reader see and explore the whole network of influence. Its centre is the digital information ecosystem. Offline events and actions (funding, events, recruitment, pressure, legislation) are in scope when they cascade into that ecosystem, and each is recorded with the path by which it does.

The early sketch put everything on one "influence methods" axis. That axis mixes five independent dimensions; separating them is the core design decision:

| Dimension | Question it answers | Examples from the sketch |
|---|---|---|
| Actor | Who acts? | Murdoch, Sinclair, Bezos/WaPo, Musk/X, RT, Havas |
| Mechanism | How? | Framing, sanewashing, dataset bias, ranking, paid influencers |
| Layer | Where in the chain? | Ownership, newsroom, platform, LLM, crowd |
| Effect | What does it do to a mind? | Filter bubbles, polarization, distorted perception |
| Case | When did it really happen? | WaPo opinion overhaul, Grok tuning, Tenet Media, Hanover Institute |

A **case** recombines the other four as an ordered path: actor → mechanism → layer → … → screen → effect. That path is what the 3D atlas will animate.

## Frameworks we build on

No single framework covers the whole chain, so the Atlas borrows each for the layer it describes best, reusing their terms and IDs.

| Framework | Contributes | Layers |
|---|---|---|
| [DISARM](https://www.disarm.foundation/faqs) (after MITRE ATT&CK) | Catalogue of influence-operation tactics and techniques (TTPs) with IDs, content-agnostic | L2–L5 |
| ABC → ABCDE (François; Pamment) | Actors, Behaviours, Content, Degree, Effect as axes of analysis | All |
| Information Disorder (Wardle & Derakhshan) | Phases create → produce → distribute; mis/dis/mal-information by falsity × intent | L2–L3 |
| Propaganda model (Herman & Chomsky) | Five structural filters: ownership, advertising, sourcing, flak, ideology | L1 |
| Framing (Entman) and agenda-setting (McCombs & Shaw) | How selection and salience shape interpretation | L2 |
| Invisible Rulers (DiResta) | Influencers, algorithms and crowds as one system; bespoke realities; asymmetry of passion | L4–L5 |
| Our Minds Under Siege (Giussani) | Technologies of influence targeting attention, perception and autonomy; technology as architecture | L6–L7 |
| Network Propaganda (Benkler, Faris, Roberts) | Empirical measurement of asymmetric media ecosystems | L1–L5 |
| Full-spectrum propaganda (DiResta & Goldstein) | A state's channels as one system on two axes: overt or covert, broadcast or social; inauthentic amplification and deceptive sourcing | L2–L5 |
| Participatory disinformation (Starbird) | Crowds co-produce narratives with elites | L5 |
| Computational propaganda (Woolley & Howard) | Bots and automated amplification | L5 |

Giussani's chapter-level list of attack vectors is not yet mapped (open question).

## The pipeline: world to screen

The "where" dimension is a seven-layer pipeline; it becomes the depth axis of the 3D scene, from the phone (L6/L7 side) back to structure (L1). (The doc embeds a diagram widget; it is described here in text.)

- **L1 Structure**: ownership, funding, advertiser dependence, state/commercial control.
- **L2 Production**: what gets written, framed, laundered, or manufactured (articles, fake think tanks, op-eds, synthetic media).
- **L3 Entry**: how content gets into a platform: direct posting, or web publication that is later scraped (SEO/GEO).
- **L4 Mediation**: ranking, search, moderation, ads; for LLMs: training corpus, retrieval, owner tuning, output loop.
- **L5 Crowd**: users, influencers, bots, brigades re-amplifying.
- **L6 Interface**: the phone UI: feed, notifications, infinite scroll, chat answers.
- **L7 Mind**: attention, perception, autonomy, trust, polarization, and the behaviour that follows.

Three feedback loops are modelled:

- **Crowd → ranking (L5 → L4).** Crowd engagement is a ranking input (DiResta: "if you make it trend, you make it true"). It works in both directions: likes and shares boost, while reports, blocks and mutes can bury an account (E114), which makes organised mass reporting a way to suppress someone (E105).
- **AI output → web (L4 → L3).** Content generated by an AI model is published and then crawled like any other, so it can be cited by a chatbot or absorbed into a later model. The log documents AI output being published at scale (E37, E55, E95); no source yet documents a model ingesting it, so this loop still needs a source.
- **Audience → production (L7 → L2)** (added 13 Nov 2026, on trial). What audiences reward shapes what producers make next, with nobody directing it (E47, E70). The evidence is about clicks and earnings, not belief.

How the sketch maps: the five Herman & Chomsky filters and ownership are L1; framing, op-eds and sanewashing are L2; the preference-based effects model is a demand-side theory linking L2 to L5; algorithmic editorialisation is L4. GenAI splits in two: dataset bias is L3→L4 via scraping, while ideological tuning (Grok) is an L1 owner acting directly on L4.

## Attributes: mundane vs adversarial

The same mechanism can be benign or hostile; the difference lives in four attributes attached to each *use* of a mechanism, not to the mechanism itself. A recommender optimising watch time and a state-funded GEO campaign both act on L4; their attributes differ.

| Attribute | Values |
|---|---|
| Intent | structural (incidental to a business model) · commercial (done for payment or profit) · ideological/editorial · adversarial (influence operation) · not established |
| Transparency | overt · undisclosed · deceptive (fronts, fake personas) |
| Coordination | individual · organisational · networked |
| Origin | domestic · foreign |

Example: a chatbot repeating propaganda because it is all over the web (structural) is a different case from one deliberately tuned to do so (ideological), even though both read as "LLM bias".

"Not established" is a value in its own right. When a ranking or moderation system produces an uneven outcome, sources often cannot say whether anyone decided it should (Brazil 2026, E102–E112; Twitter 2018, E116–E117). The Atlas records the outcome and leaves the intent open instead of choosing between "just the business model" and "someone ordered this".

The attributes are part of a case's description and source details. They are not drawn in the 3D model.

**Suppression** (the `supp.*` family, added 13 Nov 2026). The other mechanisms add or promote content; these remove it or keep it from appearing. Boundary with `plat.moderation`: moderation is a platform's routine enforcement of its own rules, and its gaps; `supp.lock_ban` is the freezing of specific content or removal of specific voices; `supp.demotion` leaves the content up but makes it harder to find, often without telling the author. Shutdowns are recorded under Extended forms of influence below.

The four `llm.*` entries came out of the Israeli cases: Pravda targets the training corpus, while Hanover and Clock Tower X target retrieval, a distinct and faster mechanism.

**Fast and slow routes into AI answers.** Retrieval (`llm.retrieval`) works within days and is well evidenced: chatbots cited the Hanover Institute in tests (E04), and their answers changed again within weeks of the press coverage (E10). Training (`llm.corpus`) is a long-term strategy and is evidenced only up to the seeding: sites built for machines, not readers (E19, E25), present in a crawl dataset used for training (E18). No source in the log yet shows a trained model's answers changing because of seeded data; the NewsGuard audit (E24) does not separate the two routes. Both routes follow the same path in the 3D model; the difference in speed and persistence is explained in the case text.

## How the 3D atlas draws the model

Decided across 13–14 Nov 2026 and built on the `feat/atlas-explore` branch.

**One atlas, always whole** (decided 14 Nov 2026). Every sphere and every actor that is drawn is in the scene at all times, each in a fixed place worked out once from all the data. Exploring an app, a case, a narrative or an owner never adds, removes or moves one of them. It only changes what is lit and where the camera looks: what is chosen takes its colour, what belongs to the app being viewed stays at full strength, and everything else stays in place, drawn pale.

**One platform at a time** (decided 14 Nov 2026). A single platform stands behind the phone: the app being viewed. It changes with what is viewed, and the links running into it are that app's. Showing several platforms at once, for content or a narrative that spreads across them, is an idea held for later.

**Checks are not drawn** (decided 14 Nov 2026). Countervailing steps (reporting, research, regulators, courts) and the actors who appear only as the ones doing the checking are left out of the scene. Each case lists its checks at the end of its panel, before the sources.

| In the model | In the scene |
|---|---|
| Layers | Depth behind the phone. The phone is the interface (L6); the platform cube behind it is mediation (L4); entry (L3) is a "direct posting" slab and a "web" sphere behind the platform; then the crowd (L5), production (L2) and structure (L1), furthest back. A ruler on the floor names the bands. |
| The app being viewed | The platform behind the phone: its ranking systems dock onto it and beams run from it to the screen. Choosing another app closes it and reopens it as that app's platform, in the same place. |
| Actors | Cubes, grouped into spheres by kind. Each sphere has a fixed place: its band sets its depth, and spheres in the same band are stacked one above the other. Companies and individuals are not grouped: they sit at the back, beside what they own or fund. |
| Fronts and fake accounts | Hollow cubes. |
| Checks and oversight | Not drawn. Regulators, courts, researchers, monitors, fact-checkers and reporters who appear only as the ones checking are listed in the case's panel. (`regulator` stays out of "States and politics", where the first grouping had put it.) |
| A case | A path drawn in pink from actor to actor to the platform and on to the screen, played step by step. A case that ran on several platforms is shown on one of them at a time. |
| A narrative | The steps that carry it, in violet. Choosing a narrative opens one of the apps it is carried on. |
| Ownership | Everything an owner controls, joined in black. Of the platforms it owns, the one being viewed is shown. |
| Evidence, grades, who knew, cost, responses | Not drawn. They are in the panel beside the scene, under each step and in the sources list. |

The visitor chooses a case, a narrative or an owner from a panel; the scene responds, and the camera moves to what was chosen. Clicking a cube opens that actor. The Influence Facts sheet on the phone counts cases and sources per line for the app, and reads "Not yet examined" where the atlas holds none.

## Data model

Nine record types: seven describe the world, Relation connects actors, and Case is an evidenced path through all of them. It replaces the app's entities/concepts/links JSON when the atlas is wired to the model (Phase 2).

```jsonc
Layer     { id, order, name }
Actor     { id, name, kind, country,
            mainstream?: bool,        // an established news institution (see below); not a measure of reach
            reach?: { figure, source },  // audience size as a source states it
            authentic: bool,          // false for fronts (Hanover, Pravda sites)
            front_for?: actorId }
          // kind: state | regulator | platform | publisher | outlet | think_tank | pr_firm
          //       | influence_vendor | content_farm | influencer | crowd | company | individual
          //       | fact_checker | researcher | monitor | political
          // political: parties, campaigns and politicians acting politically; a government acting officially is `state`
Mechanism { id, name, layer, family, refs[], definition }
Venue     { platform, surface: feed | search | chat | broadcast | print | notification }
Effect    { id, target: attention | perception | autonomy | trust | polarization | behaviour | wellbeing,
            level: proximate | ultimate, criterion,
            metric? }   // the measure behind it, quoted from the source ("4.7 points more likely to…")
          // behaviour: what people do offline (rallies, riots, votes)
          // wellbeing: mental health, body image and safety, especially of young users
          // proximate: what the machine or feed shows; ultimate: what people come to believe or do
Narrative { id, title, summary, named_by[] }   // a storyline in circulation; named_by = sources that name it
Source    { url, publisher?: actorId, date, type: fara | indictment | court | academic | journalism
            | platform_report | vendor_claim }

Relation  { source, target, kind, covert: bool, weight, sources[] }
          // kind: owns | funds | pays | contracts | directs | staffs | syndicates_to
          //       | publishes_on | scraped_by | amplifies | lobbies | regulates

Case      { id, title, period, summary,
            countries[]?, languages[]?,     // where it happened and in which languages
            attributes: { intent, transparency, coordination, origin },
            target?: public | community | decision_maker | ai_model,   // whom the case aims at
            attribution?: { by, method },   // who identified the actor behind it, and how
                                            // (filing, leak, research lab, platform report, official statement)
            steps: [{ order, actor, mechanism, layer, venue?, enabled_by?, target?,
                      narrative?, knowing?, cost?, confidence, sources[] }],
          // knowing: yes | no | disputed | unknown: whether the actor knew whose message it carried
          // cost: what the step cost, as a source states it ("$250–700 per article")
            effects[] }
```

Nine design choices matter:

- **Confidence is per step, not per case.** In the Hanover case the funding is documented in FARA filings, the mechanism by site analysis, and the effect only by journalists' retrieval tests.
- **Contract chains are first-class.** State → agency → PR firm → vendor is a chain of `contracts`/`pays` relations, so the atlas can show money flowing down to the mechanism.
- **One actor taxonomy.** `Actor.kind` is the only classification of actors; the 3D atlas groups actors into spheres by kind (states and politics; platforms; media; think tanks and fronts; influence industry; voices) instead of keeping a separate list of concepts. An actor has one kind, classified by what it is. "Mainstream" is a flag on an actor, not a group. It means an established news institution of the traditional kind: a broadcaster, newspaper, wire agency or their websites, with a newsroom and an editorial structure. It does not mean large. An independent creator with a very large audience, such as the Joe Rogan Experience, is an `influencer` with its `reach` recorded, not mainstream media (decided 14 Nov 2026, replacing the 9 Nov decision).
- **Ownership is a relation, shown as a highlight.** There is no "owner" kind and no owner sphere. An owner is any actor with `owns` relations; selecting it highlights everything it owns or controls. Ownership and narrative are different things that are both shown as highlights: ownership is a relation between actors and says who controls what; a narrative is a label on case steps and says what message a path carries, without implying the actors are coordinated. The two must be drawn differently.
- **Sources and countervailing actors are actors too.** A news source is recorded as an actor, with its owner, when its ownership or interest bears on a claim it makes or when it acts in a case (Al Jazeera and Qatar, UATV and Ukraine). Fact-checkers, researchers, monitors, regulators and journalists can be steps in a case, so the model can show influence being checked, and the checkers being weakened.
- **Whether an actor knew is recorded, not drawn.** Many intermediaries carried a message without knowing its origin (Tenet's influencers, West African outlets, jailed Chadian journalists). Each step can say so in `knowing`, as the sources report it. Like grades and responses, it belongs to the source details a reader can open, not to the picture.
- **Place and language are recorded.** A platform's protections differ by country and language: no Burmese-speaking staff during the Rohingya crisis (E145), an AI tool restricted for Brazilian phone numbers only (E111), an audit power that exists in the EU and few other places (E73). A case records its countries and languages. They are not drawn, but they would let a visitor explore by country.
- **Narratives link paths that share no contract.** A step can name the narrative it carries, so paths from unrelated actors (voice-cloned news clips, paid articles, a conference) can be explored together. A narrative is a label over existing paths, not an actor.
- **Effects feed the Influence Facts sheet.** A platform's indicators derive from the evidence in its cases, not from hand scoring (method still open; see The Influence Facts sheet).

## Exploring the atlas

A visitor explores through three selections: a **case** (an example of influence, played as a path), a **narrative** and an **owner**. These are chosen from a dedicated interface control, not by clicking 3D objects. The 3D scene responds by changing colours and highlights. The control and its look are Phase 2 work; the model's job is to supply the three lists and what each selection covers.

**Each case comes with a plain-text explanation.** What makes an operation distinctive is told in words next to the picture, drawn from the evidence, instead of by adding special shapes or categories. Example: Wikipedia, a large news outlet and Reddit are all places where content is published; what differs is how heavily search engines and AI models draw on them. That weight is explained in the case text and carried by the existing paths into search (`plat.search`) and model training (`llm.corpus`), not by a separate kind of actor.

**Evidence is the appendix, not part of the picture** (decided 13 Nov 2026). Confidence grades and source details are not encoded visually in the 3D scene. They are the record behind it: for anything shown, a reader who wants that depth can open the sources that back it, each with its grade, what the grade means, and how the source was read and analysed.

## The Influence Facts sheet

Tapping an app on the phone opens its Influence Facts sheet. It has ten lines in two parts (decided 14 Nov 2026; replaces the earlier six criteria in three groups).

| Part | Line | What it covers | Model term |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it does to you | Attention | Design that fragments focus: infinite feeds, short loops, autoplay | effect: attention |
| | Compulsive use | Being kept on the app, or steered in your choices, against your own intent | effect: autonomy |
| | Reality warping | What you come to believe is true, common or important, including what is hidden from you | effect: perception |
| | Division | Narrowed exposure to other views; people pushed apart | effect: polarization |
| | Trust | Losing the ability to tell what or who is real | effect: trust |
| | Offline consequences | Influence that spills into the physical world: rallies, harassment, votes | effect: behaviour |
| | Wellbeing | Harm to mental health, body image and safety, especially for young users | effect: wellbeing |
| What drives it | Money | How the platform earns, and what that rewards | L1 funding mechanisms |
| | Owner influence | Who controls it, and whether they steer it | `owns` relations; `own.*`, `llm.owner_tuning` |
| | Transparency | Whether outsiders can see inside: data for researchers, audits, reports | `supp.data_access`; oversight actors |

Each line's indicator is computed from the evidence, not scored by hand. How it is computed is still open: a count of cases would measure how much the Atlas has collected about a platform, not the platform itself, and a platform nobody has examined must read as "not yet examined", not as harmless. Wellbeing was added once the evidence made it the most adjudicated harm in the log (E151, E160–E163, E169–E171); its cause, the recommendation system and the design, is within scope.

Coverage is uneven and that is accepted for now: Meta has far more evidence than any other platform, which partly reflects its size and record and partly how much it has been sued and studied. More platforms are to be covered before the indicators are compared across them.

## Cases

The cases are encoded in `../data/cases.json` (24 as of 14 Nov 2026), with their actors in `actors.json` and standing relations in `relations.json`. Each case is one record: a plain-text summary, its attributes, an ordered list of steps (actor, mechanism, whom it acts on, the evidence rows behind it), its effects mapped to Influence Facts lines, and what is not established. `scripts/validate-model.mjs` checks every reference.

Choices made while encoding, still to be reviewed: one record per case, with no sub-cases and no dates on steps (the parked questions on case size and time); a countervailing step (reporting, research, a regulator or court) is marked `check: true` and needs no mechanism; a step may end at `screen`, meaning what the person holding the phone sees.

### Hanover Institute in detail

The [Guardian's analysis](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/26/fake-thinktank-israel-ai-propaganda) shows how one case exercises every layer of the model:

- **L1 chain.** Israel's government ad agency LaPam contracts Havas Media Germany, which pays five US firms registered under FARA, among them Clock Tower X ($15M, Oct 2025 to Mar 2026), Targeted Communications Global ($9.9M) and Piro ($1M). Havas's US arm deregistered on 31 Oct 2025, so the full campaign appears only in the contractors' separate filings (fund.disclosure_gap).
- **L2 content.** 124 reports and 560,000 words in nine days. All but one headline is phrased as a chatbot question. Framing devices recur: "no court has ruled" in 20 reports, "a party to the events" in 55, and one 2022 study cited 454 times across 88 reports.
- **L3 entry.** Built on Res, a GEO vendor, with a standard llms.txt file; Res's founder registered as Piro's subcontractor. The disclosure appears only in the site footer.
- **L4 two routes.** Retrieval (cited by ChatGPT and Perplexity in tests) and training data via Common Crawl, where Clock Tower X's sites already appear.
- **Countervailing loop.** After the reporting, ChatGPT's answers began flagging the institute's funding controversy and linking Quincy's coverage. Journalism re-enters the same retrieval layer.
- **Effect gap.** Researchers say it moves chatbot answers, but US polling on Israel kept declining. The model therefore separates the *proximate* effect (what the machine says) from the *ultimate* effect (what people believe).
- **Disclosed contract, undisclosed purpose.** Politico broke the story on 14 Aug 2026. Piro's FARA filings describe "informational materials" and do not mention LLMs, whereas Clock Tower X's contract names "GPT framing" outright. The model records this as a transparency value distinct from both overt and covert.

The same Havas chain also funded an overt social campaign (Freedom Not Terror, marked "Paid for by LAPAM"), so one funder can run deceptive and overt cases in parallel. ## Evidence and neutrality rules

The Atlas is credible only if every edge can be checked and the catalogue does not pick sides.

1. Every Case step carries at least one Source, and every evidence row carries exactly one confidence grade:

   | Grade | Meaning |
   |---|---|
   | documented | Filings, court records, platform reports, peer-reviewed studies, authenticated leaks |
   | reported | Investigative journalism, reproducible tests, research cited in journalism |
   | claimed | An actor's or vendor's own statement, unverified |
   | contested | Credible accounts conflict |
   | opinion | Commentary or argument |

   A row also records how its source was read (`read_status`: full, partial, secondhand, unread), an optional `note` for any part graded differently, and the `responses` of the people and organisations named (denial, explanation, admission, no reply). How sources are read and graded is set out in `source-analysis.md`.
2. Mechanisms are ideology-agnostic. Cases should span actor origins (Russian, Chinese, Iranian, Israeli, US government and private) and ownership directives across the political spectrum.
3. Distinguish layers precisely: a social-media influence operation (L5, e.g. STOIC) is not LLM poisoning (L4, e.g. Hanover).
4. Name intermediaries without implying culpability unless a source does (e.g. Tenet's influencers).
5. Prefer primary sources (FARA filings, court documents, platform transparency reports) over syndications.
6. A narrative is recorded only when a source names it as one, and it is described as a claim in circulation. The Atlas does not label narratives true or false; the sources attached to each step carry that.
7. Evidence of effect outweighs evidence of capability. A case states what effect is evidenced and says plainly when none is ("$15M was spent; no effect on opinion is shown"). An operator's or vendor's own account of its success is graded "claimed" (E21, E91).
8. A fact and the source cited for it must match. Each row is checked against the passage it rests on; anything the source does not contain is removed or marked as coming from elsewhere (see `source-analysis.md`).
